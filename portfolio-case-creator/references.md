# References and applied reading

This document brings together public references and additional guidance for creating portfolio case studies. Use it to assess clarity, evidence, and individual contribution; define the content and visual direction based on the current project.

## Project-independent application

- Open with the problem, audience, role, team, timeframe, and status of the work.
- Connect decisions to available evidence and explain alternatives and constraints.
- Give metrics context: source, method, sample, timeframe, and limitations. Separate expectations from observed results.
- Show how research and feedback informed changes without inventing validation or impact.
- Choose artifacts based on what they help explain, and keep them legible with useful captions.
- Define colors, typography, composition, and visual elements according to the project's authorized identity and accessibility requirements.
- Treat external examples as communication references; do not transfer names, screens, numbers, authorship, or visual identities into the user's case study.

## UX Collective Brasil article

[How to structure a UX/UI portfolio case study and impress recruiters (in Portuguese)](https://brasil.uxdesign.cc/como-estruturar-um-case-para-portf%C3%B3lio-de-ux-ui-e-impressionar-recrutadores-62266ee50839), by Leandro Rezende, 2018.

The article argues that portfolios should not be limited to aesthetics or the final interface. A case study should show an understanding of real needs, connect them to business goals, consider alternatives that fit the context, and explain how to evaluate whether the solution works. It also emphasizes product thinking, consistency and scalability, structured communication, and collaboration.

Application in this skill: provide audience and business context, show the process and decisions, record feasibility and trade-offs, explain how success will be evaluated, and present the work so another person can understand it and continue from it.

## Google article

[Google UX Design Portfolio Tips](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/ux_design_portfolio_tips_19.pdf).

The guide recommends presenting three to five case studies that demonstrate variety and end-to-end process; clearly stating role, impact, and cross-functional collaboration; combining images with explanations; providing enough context for independent reading; and reviewing navigation, clarity of design thinking, and visual quality.

Application in this skill: include role and team details, tell the journey to the solution, combine text with artifacts, and check that the case study can be understood without a live presentation. The recommendation about variety applies to the portfolio as a whole; each case study should remain focused on one problem and one story.

## Staff and Principal benchmarks

The examples below are public accounts in personal portfolios. They show ways to communicate scope and influence; their reported outcomes have not been independently audited and should not be used as numerical benchmarks.

- [Jessica Harllee — Etsy Web Toolkit](https://jessicaharllee.com/work/etsy-web-toolkit/): explains a systemic problem, individual contributions in design and front-end development, documentation, facilitation, coaching, adoption beyond the original team, and the evolution into a dedicated team. A reference for multiplier effects and system adoption.
- [Jolie Godin — North Star for a fragmented platform](https://joliegodin.com/north-star-ai): identifies a Senior Staff role, reach across 15 product areas, executive alignment, shared principles and architecture, and an effect on the roadmap. A reference for cross-functional strategy and reducing ambiguity.
- [Lara Sanchez — Design Ops at Viator](https://larasanchez.com/portfolio/leading-design-ops-at-viator): describes direction, processes, Design and Engineering collaboration, systems for different sides of a marketplace, and team development. A reference for organizational capability and practice leadership.

Summary: Staff and Principal case studies often need to make influence beyond a single deliverable explicit, while still showing craft, concrete decisions, and connections to people's problems. Use the target level to select and emphasize evidence; do not inflate the scope of smaller projects.
